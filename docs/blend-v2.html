§1 · The rule

The path is the name of the test

One rule generates the whole set, and it is the only thing worth memorising.

Today an endpoint id says where it sits in a taxonomy and its path says what resource it returns. Neither says what is being measured. nf.dashboard serves /dashboard; the family note says it exercises "a deeper middleware chain", which you cannot recover from either string. jr.product serves /products/{product} and pp.cust_orders serves /customers/{customer}/orders; they sit in different families but both are a GET with a path capture, and nothing explains the split.

In v2 the first path segment names the thing under test and the rest names the arm. /compressed/medium is the medium payload on the compressing route. /parameters/{one}/with-second/{two} is the router carrying two captures. The id is the same words with a dot, plus the arm where one route carries two, so compressed.gzip_medium and parameters.two need no lookup table.

The rule that makes the names honest is the one underneath: every feature endpoint is a baseline endpoint plus exactly one factor. The response payload is a controlled variable with three fixed values, and a feature family reuses one of them unchanged. Subtracting the base from the arm leaves the feature and nothing else.

BaseAdd one factorEndpoint /json/small+ bearer token/authorized/small /json/medium+ gzip -6/compressed/medium /json/large+ ETag/cached/large /cached/large+ If-None-Match304, no body /json/small+ two captures/parameters/{one}/… /json/small+ 16 no-op layers/middleware/sixteen /body/bind/small+ validation/body/validate/small

Two consequences are worth stating before the set, because they look like mistakes otherwise. Some endpoints share a path and differ only in the request: authorized.allowed and authorized.denied are both GET /authorized/small, separated by whether the bearer token is the right one. That is deliberate. Routing cost has to be identical for the delta to be the authorization outcome, and the only way to hold routing constant is to use the same route. The id carries the distinction, and spec/plan.json already supports two endpoints over one path — bv.order and bv.rejected both post to /orders/validate today.

The other is that features get their own paths rather than riding on the JSON routes. /compressed/small exists instead of sending accept-encoding: gzip to /json/small, because compression wired globally puts a "did they ask?" check on every response in the blend and contaminates the baseline it is measured against. Feature wiring is scoped to the feature's routes.

Finally, being in the set and being in an aggregate are separated. Two endpoints render templates, and templates cannot be ranked across languages — Thymeleaf, Handlebars and html/template are different products. They are sampled exactly like everything else, on the same process microseconds from their own comparison point, and they are simply absent from the weight vector of any blend meant to rank. §5 is how that works.

§2 · Payloads

Three responses, and everything reuses them

The payload is the controlled variable. It has three values and they are generated, committed and identical in every language.

harness/make_fixture.py gains a payloads key holding all three, built from the same seeded generator that already produces the products and orders. Each is an object with a fixed envelope and an array of records, so the three differ in record count and in nothing else. Shape is deliberately not crossed with size here; a flat-versus-nested pair would need its own arm and does not exist in v2.

PayloadRecordsBytesgzip −6RatioRegime it sits in
small11251251.00One write. Fits any buffer. Compressing it is pure loss.
medium898,1311,3920.17Inside a default 16 KiB socket buffer, past any stack buffer.
large1,425131,36018,8960.14Several writes. Framing and partial-write handling show up.

Record counts are the pinned quantity; the byte counts are what those counts produce and are stated so a reader can check content-length against them. They were computed against the proposed generator, not measured from a target. Sizes sit safely inside a regime rather than on a boundary — probing where the boundary is needs more than three points and is a different question.

The records are distinct rather than fifty fixture products cycled. Cycling compresses 26:1 and would make gzip look free; distinct records land at 7:1, which is ordinary for JSON. The small payload is the one that earns its slot twice over: gzip −6 turns 125 bytes into 125 bytes, so compressed.gzip_small minus compressed.identity_small is the cost a framework pays to achieve nothing, which is exactly the threshold behaviour worth exposing.

Request bodies use small and medium only. A 131 KB request body at rung 5 would move 1.5 GB/s across loopback and measure the kernel. Response bodies do use large: json.large, compressed.identity_large and cached.large together move about 110 MB/s at rung 5, which loopback carries comfortably and which is the first thing to check if rung 5 ever looks strange.

Templates are the case that separating sampling from weighting was for. docs/plan.html keeps them out of the blend because they "would swamp a blended aggregate with their own response sizes", and because the row is partly an engine benchmark: Thymeleaf, Razor, Handlebars, html/template and Tera are separate products, so the number has to be reported with the engine named. Both objections are about the aggregate, not the measurement. Suite B answers them by moving templates to a separate night, where the two arms sit minutes apart on one process. Sampling them evenly and leaving them out of the ranking vectors answers them better: the arms sit microseconds apart in the same stream, and the number still never reaches a ranking.

Byte-identical output across five template engines is the hard part, and forcing it would mean contorting every template. So conform.py gains a second canonicalizer: for text/html it collapses runs of whitespace before hashing, the way it already sorts keys for application/json at line 95. Content is pinned — same elements, same order, same values, generated into the fixture — and whitespace is free. If two engines still cannot agree on content, the endpoint is per-engine and the spec should say so rather than quietly ranking a Handlebars target against a Thymeleaf one.

The 500 is gone. ep.boom measured stack-trace capture policy, which is configuration rather than framework, and Suite B already runs unhandled-500 against ok-200 on one process where the comparison holds. The 403 survives as authorized.denied, where it finally has a mechanism behind it instead of being a constant-cost status write. And /health leaves the blend but keeps its route: harness/run.py:194 uses it as the readiness probe, so it is infrastructure, like /__meta, and infrastructure is not measured.

What has to be pinned, and to what

A feature endpoint is only a clean delta if the feature itself is constant across forty-three targets. Each of these is a line in the spec, not a convention.

The memoization hole gets closed at the same time

harness/plan.py:13 sets INSTANCES = 64, so each endpoint has sixty-four pre-resolved requests and no more. A target could precompute all sixty-four responses at boot and reduce every endpoint to a dispatch and a write, and conformance could not see it, because identical bytes are the whole point of the fingerprint. Raise it to 512. spec/plan.json is 89 KB today and would reach roughly 700 KB, which is cheap, and the freshness counter makes the feature families detect it outright.

§5 · Blends

Sample evenly, weight afterwards

The endpoint set is not a traffic simulation, so the request stream should not pretend to be one. Every endpoint is drawn with equal probability, and every opinion about traffic lives in a weight vector applied when the aggregate is composed.

blend-v1 gives each endpoint a share that decides two unrelated things at once: how often it is drawn, and how much it counts in the aggregate. The second falls out of the first, because gen/blend.mjs:180 sums every per-endpoint histogram into overall unconditionally. Being in the stream is being in the blend, and the weights were assigned by judgement rather than measured from any real application, so a guess about traffic is baked irreversibly into data collection.

v2 drops share and draws uniformly. The endpoint set becomes a set rather than a mix, and the arithmetic improves on its own.

Per endpoint, per runblend-v1 weightedv2 uniformConsequence

The 10,000-request floor in spec/ladder.json stops being a design constraint to check and becomes an automatic property: 1.38M requests over 45 endpoints is 30,667 each, clearing it 3.1× without anyone arranging it.

Composition simplifies too. With every n equal, scaling a histogram by w / n is scaling it by w, and merging the raw histograms untouched is the uniform blend. There is no longer a sampling field and a weighting field to keep consistent — there is one weight vector, applied at the end, and the raw data underneath it has no opinion in it at all.

uniform blend   = sum of h[i]                 no scaling; this is the data as collected
weighted blend  = sum of h[i] * w[i]          w from spec/blends.json, normalised
p50 … p99       = percentiles of the merged histogram
                                              published as they are; nothing divides them

Families end up weighted by how many endpoints they hold — domain at 8 of 42, headers at 2 — which is the right default, because a family with more arms is a family testing more distinct things. Anyone who disagrees says so in a weight vector rather than in the sampler.

Which blend is the headline

uniform, and this is the one recommendation in this section that is not merely mechanical.

docs/plan.html already records why: "per-endpoint latency is not independent of the mix, because changing the mix changes the JIT state that produced it. Reweighting after the fact does not recover comparability." That is true, and it applies to every weighted blend in the list below — each one describes what those endpoints did under a uniform mix, not what they would do under its own. uniform is the single blend where the approximation is zero, because it is the mix that was actually run. Publishing it as the headline also ends the argument about whether the weights flatter somebody, permanently, because there are none.

spec/blends.json then holds the weighted vectors as alternate views. harness/summarize.py has every per-endpoint histogram in memory when it writes a summary, so computing five aggregates instead of one costs a few hundred bytes and no measurement time.

Adding a blend is a pull request against a JSON file and a re-summarise of samples already on disk. No re-measurement.

The ceiling is p99 per endpoint, p99.9 for the blend

At 30,667 requests an endpoint's p99 rests on 307 observations, which is sound, and its p99.9 rests on 31, which is not. docs/plan.html already draws this line — "fine for a median, shaky for p99, and useless for p99.9" — and uniform sampling moves every endpoint to the same side of it. So per-endpoint rows publish p50, p90 and p99 and stop.

The blend is a different population. Merged across 45 endpoints it holds 1.38M samples, so its p99.9 rests on 1,380 observations. That one is real and worth keeping: rung 5 exists to watch saturation, and saturation shows up in the tail before it shows up anywhere else. Dropping p99.9 would blind the rung that the ladder climbs to reach.

What to ship so the browser can compose

Composing a blend in the browser needs the per-endpoint distributions, not their percentiles. Shipping the full 920-bucket histogram is the obvious way and the wrong one: measured against a real run, an endpoint's histogram occupies 47 buckets at a hundred samples and 354 at a few thousand, so at full-run counts it is near 400 and sparse encoding saves little. A fixed-size quantile summary is both smaller and, unlike a sparse histogram, does not grow as the distribution spreads.

Two shapes were tested against the full histograms from a real run, composing a weighted blend from the summaries and comparing it to the same blend composed from the histograms.

Mean absolute error against the composed full histogram, over nine target-and-rung pairs; the worst single case for the tail-weighted set was 1.96%. Both are base64 byte counts at three bytes per value, which holds microsecond times up to sixteen seconds.

Take the tail-weighted set: 1% steps to p90, 0.5% to p99, 0.1% to p99.9, 0.02% beyond. It costs 88 bytes more than the even one and it is the difference between a blend p99.9 that is usable and one that is off by 14%. Each point carries the width of the interval it represents as its weight, which is what makes an uneven set compose correctly — weighting uneven points evenly produces errors of several hundred percent at p90, which is how this was nearly got wrong.

The bill is small enough that no rung has to be dropped. 4.4 MB is the raw figure and nothing is served raw: git stores blobs zlib-compressed and Pages negotiates content-encoding on the wire, so the number that matters is the compressed one at both ends.

Measured by compressing the 360 real histograms in results/2026-09-13T2254Z.go.2b3f0e.jsonl and scaling per-histogram to a full run of 9,030. Delta encoding losing to plain integers is the result worth keeping: latency values recur heavily across endpoints and targets, which gives the compressor long repeated matches, and differencing destroys exactly those matches to save bytes the compressor was already removing. Hand-rolled compaction ahead of a real compressor made the file 19% bigger.

So: plain JSON integer arrays, all five rungs, committed uncompressed. Roughly 1 MB per run at rest and on the wire, against a current summary of 47 KB for six targets and about 335 KB at forty-three. Brotli saves a further 15% and is not worth a manual compression step — DecompressionStream handles gzip and deflate but not brotli, and leaving the file plain means Pages negotiates the encoding and the page just calls fetch().json(). This also matches what harness/summarize.py already says about its own output: nothing is dropped for size, because the repository is public and neither storage nor Actions minutes is billed.

§8 · Sequence

Order of work

The expensive thing is the target wiring, and it is expensive in proportion to how many targets exist when it starts.

  1. Fixture and spec first

    Payloads, ETags, token, then endpoints.json rewritten whole. Everything downstream reads these two, and they are the cheapest things in the set to change.

  2. Headers through the pipeline

    plan.py, both generators, conform.py. Until a request can carry a header, four of the thirteen families cannot exist.

  3. Uniform sampling and named blends

    blends.json, a uniform draw, and the composed aggregate in summarize.py. Worth doing early and out of order: it removes more code than it adds, touches nothing the target authors touch, and applies to blend-v1 as it stands.

  4. One target end to end

    node:fastify, the language's anchor. Its responses become the fingerprint every other Node target is gated against, so it establishes the contract the rest then have to meet with their own facilities.

  5. Then one framework per language

    Fastify, Gin, Spring Boot. These are where the feature wirings are least alike, so they will find whatever the spec left ambiguous.

  6. Then the remaining fifteen

    Mechanical once three languages have a worked example, and the point at which conformance across forty-five endpoints starts earning its keep again.

  7. Endpoint pages, the delta column, the blend selector

    base, varies and the named blends are already in the spec by then, so the site can state "costs 41 µs over json.medium" as generated text rather than leaving the reader to subtract, and can offer five aggregates rather than one.

  8. The arbitrary composer

    Quantile summaries into the summary file, then the endpoint selector in the browser. Independent of all of the above and cheap enough that it need not wait for any of it.
